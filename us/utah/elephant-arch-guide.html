Elephant Arch, Washington Utah: The Complete Trail Guide

A short hike on paper and a genuinely demanding one underfoot. Elephant Arch sits in the Red Cliffs Desert Reserve in Washington County, southwest Utah — roughly ten minutes from St. George and nowhere near Moab, despite what half the search results suggest. The route is unsigned, deep with sand, and completely without shade.

By Sam Okoye Published Aug 11, 2026 Updated Aug 28, 2026 11 min read

Location Clarification: This Is Not Moab

Elephant Arch is in the Red Cliffs Desert Reserve, Washington County, Utah, accessed from the city of Washington on the eastern edge of the St. George metro area. It is not in Arches National Park, and it is not the Elephant Butte or Elephant Hill formations near Moab, which are approximately 300 miles northeast. Searches for “elephant arch washington utah” regularly return Moab results — check the county before you drive.

Where Elephant Arch Actually Is

The Red Cliffs Desert Reserve covers about 62,000 acres of Navajo sandstone north of the St. George metro area. It exists primarily as protected habitat for the Mojave desert tortoise, which is why access is deliberately low-key: few signs, no visitor centre, and trailheads that look like residential street parking.

Elephant Arch sits in the reserve’s eastern section, reached from the Cottonwood Springs area on the north edge of the city of Washington. The formation gets its name from a profile that reads convincingly as an elephant’s head and trunk from one specific angle — and not at all from most others.

Why the location confusion happens

Three separate naming collisions send hikers to the wrong end of the state:

  • Washington, Utah vs. Washington State — the city shares its name with the state, so searches split across both.
  • Elephant Butte — a well-known formation near Moab, 300 miles northeast.
  • Elephant Hill — a notorious 4WD route in the Needles district of Canyonlands, also near Moab.

If your mapping app routes you toward Moab, Green River or I-70, you have the wrong feature. The correct drive from St. George is under fifteen minutes on surface streets.

Getting There and Parking

From St. George, take I-15 north to Exit 10 (Washington Parkway), then follow Buena Vista Boulevard north toward the reserve boundary. The trailhead parking is a dirt pull-out at the end of the residential grid — unmarked, with room for roughly eight vehicles.

Parking Reality Check

The lot fills by 8 a.m. on winter weekends. Overflow parking on the residential streets is legal but unpopular with residents; park considerately and do not block driveways. A standard passenger car reaches the trailhead fine — the dirt section is short and graded.

The Route, Step by Step

Section 1 — Trailhead to the wash (0.0–0.7 mi)

The opening stretch follows a firm doubletrack heading northeast. This is the easy part, and it is misleading. Stay left at the first two junctions; the right forks lead into the wider Cottonwood Springs trail network and add several miles.

Section 2 — The sand wash (0.7–2.1 mi)

The route drops into a broad sandy wash and stays in it for well over a mile. This is where the hike earns its moderate rating. The sand is deep, loose and unconsolidated — the kind that swallows a boot to the ankle and roughly doubles your perceived effort.

Expect this section to take considerably longer than the distance suggests. Two miles per hour is optimistic here; 1.2 to 1.5 is realistic for most hikers.

Section 3 — The scramble to the arch (2.1–1.7 mi from start, final approach)

Where the wash narrows and bends north, an unmarked use-trail climbs left onto slickrock. Missing this turn is the single most common error, and it sends people a mile up the wrong drainage. Look for a worn sandstone ramp with a small cairn — cairns here are unofficial and periodically get knocked down.

The final 300 yards involve mild scrambling on Navajo sandstone. No exposure, no technical moves, but smooth soles will slip.

Navigation Warning

This route is unsigned from end to end. There is no trail marker naming Elephant Arch anywhere along it. Download an offline map before you leave the car — cell coverage in the wash is intermittent at best, and the wash junctions look identical in both directions on the return.

Best Time to Hike

October through April, without qualification. The Red Cliffs reserve is low desert, and summer surface temperatures on the sandstone regularly exceed 120°F. There is no shade on any part of this route.

What to Bring

  • Three litres of water per person — there is no water source and no shade for the entire route.
  • Gaiters — unglamorous but genuinely useful; the wash fills boots with sand within minutes.
  • Offline map — the route is unsigned and the junctions are ambiguous.
  • Sun hat and long sleeves — reflected heat off pale sandstone is brutal by mid-morning.
  • Grippy soles — the final scramble is smooth rock.

Desert Tortoise Protocol

The Red Cliffs Desert Reserve exists because of the Mojave desert tortoise, a federally threatened species. Encounters are uncommon but real, particularly in spring mornings.

If you see one: stop, keep your distance, and never pick it up. A startled tortoise voids its bladder as a defence response, and in this climate that water loss can be fatal to the animal. Stay on established surfaces — the cryptobiotic soil crust between routes takes decades to recover from a single footprint.

Is Elephant Arch Worth It?

Honestly: it depends on what you want. The arch itself is modest by Utah standards — nothing approaching Delicate Arch or Landscape Arch in scale. What it offers instead is solitude within fifteen minutes of a city of 100,000 people. On a January weekday you may well have it entirely to yourself.

If you are based in St. George for a Zion trip and want a half-day hike that no tour bus will ever reach, this is a strong choice. If you are travelling specifically to photograph a famous arch, drive to Moab.

Pair It With Zion

St. George sits 50 minutes from Zion’s Springdale entrance. Elephant Arch works well as an arrival-day or departure-day hike when Zion permits fall through — or as an acclimatisation walk before a bigger canyon day.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where is Elephant Arch in Utah?

In the Red Cliffs Desert Reserve, Washington County, southwest Utah, accessed from the city of Washington near St. George. It is roughly 300 miles from Moab and is not inside Arches National Park.

Do you need a permit for Elephant Arch?

No. There is no permit, no reservation and no entrance fee. The reserve is managed jointly by the BLM and Washington County with open public access.

How hard is the Elephant Arch trail?

Moderate. The 3.4-mile round trip has modest elevation gain, but more than a mile of deep loose sand and an unsigned final scramble make it harder than the numbers imply. Budget two to three hours.

Is Elephant Arch the same as Elephant Butte near Moab?

No. Elephant Butte and Elephant Hill are both near Moab in eastern Utah. Elephant Arch is in Washington County in the southwest corner of the state.

Can you hike Elephant Arch in summer?

You can, but we recommend against it. There is no shade anywhere on the route and summer highs exceed 100°F, with sandstone surface temperatures far higher. October through April is the sensible window.
