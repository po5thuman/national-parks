Canyonlands Camping & The Maze: The Complete 2026 Backcountry Guide

Thirty-eight developed campsites across a park of 337,570 acres — and beyond them, some of the most demanding backcountry in the United States. Here is how to get a pitch, how the Recreation.gov permit system actually works, and what a self-supported trip into The Maze genuinely requires.

Camping in Canyonlands: The Short Version

Canyonlands has thirty-eight developed campsites. Not thirty-eight campgrounds — thirty-eight individual pitches, across a park more than five hundred square miles in size. Twelve are at Willow Flat on the Island in the Sky mesa and twenty-six at Squaw Flat in The Needles. That is the entire frontcountry inventory.

Everything else is backcountry, and backcountry here means permits, self-sufficiency and, in most cases, a capable vehicle. There is no lodge, no cabin, no restaurant and no shop inside any district. If you want a bed and a shower, you sleep in Moab or Monticello and drive in.

That scarcity shapes the whole planning process. Willow Flat cannot be reserved at all and fills before nine in the morning through spring and autumn. Squaw Flat takes reservations for part of its sites and those go months ahead. Backcountry permits for the desirable zones — Chesler Park, Elephant Canyon, the White Rim campsites — disappear within minutes of the booking window opening.

The three permit types, distinguished

Visitors conflate these constantly, and the distinctions carry citations. Park entry is $30 per vehicle for seven days, no reservation needed, no timed entry at Canyonlands. Day-use 4WD permits cost $6 via Recreation.gov and are required for vehicles, motorcycles and bicycles on White Rim Road, Elephant Hill and Lavender Canyon — even for a single afternoon. Overnight backcountry permits cost $36 non-refundable plus $5 per person per night and are required to sleep anywhere outside the two campgrounds, in every district. A day-use permit does not let you stay the night; a backcountry permit is a separate booking.

If you are still deciding which district to base yourself in, start with the Canyonlands National Park hub, which covers entry fees, visitor centers and driving times. For what to walk once you are camped, see the best hikes in Canyonlands.

Canyonlands Campgrounds Compared

Two campgrounds, two districts, two entirely different booking problems. Willow Flat is small, waterless and unreservable. Squaw Flat is larger, has seasonal water, and is partly reservable — which in practice means the reservable half vanishes early and the first-come half fills by mid-morning on spring weekends.

A note on the name

The Needles campground appears in older guidebooks and on some signage as Squaw Flat, and the National Park Service has been moving away from that term following federal action on derogatory place names. You will see both "Squaw Flat Campground" and "The Needles Campground" in official material and on Recreation.gov. They are the same place, three miles past the Needles Visitor Center.

Willow Flat is twelve sites of juniper and slickrock on the mesa top, a few minutes' drive from Green River Overlook. It is the only place to sleep legally inside the Island in the Sky district without a backcountry permit, and it has precisely one significant advantage: you wake up already on the mesa, which means Mesa Arch at sunrise is a ten-minute drive rather than a pre-dawn expedition from Moab.

Everything else about it demands preparation. There is no water — not seasonally, not sometimes, never. Every drop for drinking, cooking and washing comes in with you, and the nearest reliable tap is at the visitor center six miles away when it is running, or Moab when it is not. There are no showers, no dump station and no hookups.

Because it cannot be reserved and holds only twelve parties, the acquisition strategy is simple and unromantic: arrive early. In March through May and September through October, sites are typically claimed by nine in the morning, sometimes by eight on weekends. The practical method is to drive up the evening before, check for vacancies, and if there are none, sleep on BLM land along UT-313 and return at first light as departing campers pack up.

Warning

No water means no water

Willow Flat has no potable water supply at any time of year. Plan on one gallon (4 litres) per person per day for drinking alone, plus cooking and washing on top. A two-night stay for two people means a minimum of 16 litres in the vehicle before you leave Moab. Running out on the mesa in summer is a genuine emergency, not an inconvenience — the nearest resupply is a 40-minute drive each way.

The campground sits at around 6,000 feet, which makes summer nights tolerable and winter nights properly cold — below freezing from November through February, with the added complication that the single access road across the neck closes for snow and rockfall. Check road status before committing to a winter stay.

Squaw Flat is the better campground by a clear margin, and not only because it has water. The sites are tucked against slickrock outcrops and beneath juniper, several of them backed directly onto rock walls that give both shade and shelter from the wind. It is three miles from the visitor center, immediately adjacent to the Squaw Flat trailhead, and three miles of gravel from the Elephant Hill trailhead where the Chesler Park Loop and Druid Arch hikes begin.

That adjacency is the argument for camping here rather than driving out from Moab. Both Needles classics are eleven-mile days that want a seven o'clock start; doing them from Moab means leaving at half past five. From Squaw Flat you are at the trailhead in ten minutes.

The booking situation is split. A portion of the sites can be reserved through Recreation.gov during the spring and autumn seasons, and those are released on a rolling window and taken quickly. The remainder are first-come, first-served, and on a spring weekend they are gone by mid-morning. Two group sites, Wooden Shoe and Squaw Flat Group, are reservable separately for parties of eleven or more.

Water is seasonal — generally on from around March to October and drained for winter. When it is off, the flush toilets revert to vault and you carry everything, exactly as at Willow Flat. Confirm the current status by phone with the Needles Visitor Center before a shoulder-season trip; the switch-off date moves with the first hard freeze.

Backcountry Permits: The Recreation.gov Process

Every overnight stay outside Willow Flat and Squaw Flat requires an advance backcountry permit, in every district, without exception. That covers a tent in a Needles zone, a vehicle campsite on White Rim Road, a bivouac in The Maze and a river beach on the Colorado. There is no walk-up option and no permit desk that will issue one on the day.

Work out the arithmetic before you book, because the $36 is the same whether you are one person for one night or six people for four. Two people for three nights costs $36 plus thirty dollars in per-person fees — $66 total. A solo overnight costs $41. The fixed fee makes short solo trips expensive and longer group trips comparatively cheap.

How the booking actually goes

Permits are allocated by zone, and each zone has a hard quota — typically one to three parties per night. When the four-month window opens at the scheduled hour, the desirable zones for spring and autumn weekends are taken within minutes. Chesler Park, Elephant Canyon and the White Rim campsites are the most competitive in the park.

  • Create your Recreation.gov account in advance and save payment details. Registering during the release costs you the permit.
  • Know your zone codes before the window opens. Browsing the map while the clock runs is how people end up with nothing.
  • Have a second and third choice ready. Adjacent zones are frequently just as good and go slightly slower.
  • Consider midweek. Tuesday and Wednesday nights in the same zones are dramatically easier to secure.
  • Check for cancellations. The $36 is non-refundable, so cancellations are rare — but they happen, and inventory reappears without notice.

Warning

Day-use and overnight permits are not interchangeable

A $6 day-use permit lets you drive White Rim Road, Elephant Hill or Lavender Canyon between sunrise and sunset. It does not permit you to sleep anywhere. Staying overnight on those roads requires a separate backcountry permit at $36 plus $5 per person per night, tied to a specific numbered campsite. Rangers patrol the White Rim campsites and check permits against site assignments. Turning up at a site you have not booked means a citation and being moved on in the dark.

Full detail on which roads need the $6 day-use permit is on the Canyonlands hub. Note that neither permit type is required simply to park at the Elephant Hill trailhead and walk — day hiking anywhere in the park needs nothing beyond the entrance fee.

White Rim Road Overnight Trips

The White Rim is a hundred-mile loop around and below the Island in the Sky mesa, running along the pale sandstone bench you look down on from Grand View Point. It is the classic Canyonlands multi-day trip: two to four days by 4WD or mountain bike, descending the Shafer Trail switchbacks, tracing the rim of the inner gorge, and climbing back out via the Mineral Bottom switchbacks or Potash Road.

Twenty designated campsites are spaced along the route, each holding one to three parties depending on size. You book a specific site for a specific night, and your itinerary is fixed at the point of booking — the loop is one-way in practical terms and you cannot simply stop where you like.

Mountain bikers almost always run the loop with a support vehicle carrying water, food and camping kit, which itself needs the day-use permit if it is travelling the road. Self- supported cycling is possible but means hauling four days of water on a bike across slickrock and sand, which very few parties choose twice.

The two access descents deserve respect. The Shafer Trail drops fifteen hundred feet in a series of exposed switchbacks cut into the cliff in the 1950s for uranium hauling; it is driveable in a competent high-clearance 4WD and genuinely intimidating from above. The Mineral Bottom switchbacks on the western side are equally steep. Both become impassable when wet, and the road authority does not always sign the closure at the top.

Needles Backcountry Zones

The Needles has the best backpacking in Canyonlands and, for most people, the most approachable backcountry camping in the park. The zones are reached on foot from the Elephant Hill or Squaw Flat trailheads — no special vehicle needed — and the terrain is spectacular in a way that photographs never quite convey.

Camping in the Chesler Park and Elephant Canyon zones converts the district's two punishing day hikes into a comfortable and far superior two-day trip. You walk in during the afternoon, watch the spires turn orange and then violet at dusk, sleep under one of the darkest skies in the contiguous United States, and take Druid Arch at first light with the canyon to yourself.

  • Chesler Park zones — the most competitive in the district; sites sit at the edge of the spire-ringed basin.
  • Elephant Canyon — wash-bottom camping on the Druid Arch approach; convenient, less scenic, easier to book.
  • Big Spring and Squaw Canyon — quieter loop options with slickrock pitches.
  • Salt Creek — long approach, archaeological sites, genuine solitude.

Zone rules are strict about where you may pitch. In most Needles zones you must camp on slickrock, sand or an established site — never on cryptobiotic soil, the black living crust that takes decades to regrow after a single footfall. Fires are banned throughout the backcountry in all districts, so cooking is on a stove.

The two-day Needles plan that works

Book a Chesler Park or Elephant Canyon zone for a single night. Day one: leave the Elephant Hill trailhead after lunch, walk in four to five miles, make camp, spend the evening light in the basin. Day two: start at dawn, take Druid Arch out and back, then close the Chesler loop via the Joint Trail on the way out. Total cost for two people: $46 in permit fees. Total improvement over doing either as a day hike: enormous.

The Maze: Planning a Self-Supported Expedition

The Maze receives roughly two percent of Canyonlands visitors, and the National Park Service describes it as one of the most remote and inaccessible areas in the contiguous United States. That phrase gets used loosely elsewhere. Here it is a literal operational statement: rangers cannot reach you quickly, there is no cell coverage in the district, recovery of a stuck or broken vehicle is measured in days and thousands of dollars, and the roads out become impassable within minutes of rain.

What you get in exchange is a canyon system of extraordinary intricacy — a genuine maze of interlocking gorges below the Orange Cliffs — the Harvest Scene pictograph panel, and the near-certainty that you will not see another party for days. It is one of the few places left in the lower forty-eight where solitude is effectively guaranteed rather than merely likely.

Getting there

Access begins at Hans Flat Ranger Station, reached on 46 miles of graded dirt road from UT-24, itself about two and a half hours from Green River. A two-wheel-drive vehicle can usually manage the road to Hans Flat in dry conditions. Everything beyond it cannot.

From Hans Flat, the descent into the Maze proper runs down the Flint Trail — a series of steep, narrow, unmaintained switchbacks dropping off the Orange Cliffs. It is the crux of the entire journey. The Flint Trail is one-lane with limited passing places, closes entirely when wet or snowy, and typically stays closed through much of winter. Below it, another three to six hours of technical driving separates you from the Maze Overlook and the Doll House.

Horseshoe Canyon is the exception worth knowing about. A detached unit of the park administered with The Maze, it holds the Great Gallery — a panel of life-size Barrier Canyon Style pictographs several thousand years old and among the most significant rock art in North America. It is reached by an ordinary vehicle on dirt from UT-24 and a seven-mile round-trip day hike down into the canyon. No 4WD, no permit for day use. If The Maze itself is beyond your setup, Horseshoe Canyon is how you see a piece of it.

Minimum trip length

Three nights is the realistic floor for The Maze, and four is more comfortable. A single day is consumed getting from a paved road to a campsite, and another getting out. Parties that attempt to compress it end up driving technical terrain under time pressure, which is precisely the condition that produces rollovers and broken vehicles in a place where help is a day away.

Warning

This is not a scenic drive

The Maze is the most consequential ground in the national park system for self-sufficiency. There is no cell service anywhere in the district, no water, no fuel, no ranger patrol you can rely on and no realistic prospect of rapid rescue. Vehicle recovery from below the Flint Trail routinely costs thousands of dollars and takes days to arrange. The Park Service recommends travelling in two vehicles, carrying two full-size spare tyres, and having the genuine ability to perform your own repairs and recoveries. If you have not driven technical 4WD terrain before, The Maze is not where you learn.

4WD and Vehicle Requirements

"High-clearance four-wheel drive" is a specification, not a category of feeling. The Park Service means a vehicle with genuine low-range gearing, meaningful ground clearance, all-terrain or off-road tyres and short overhangs. A crossover SUV with all-wheel drive and road tyres does not qualify for any of the technical roads described on this page, regardless of what the badge on the tailgate says.

Rental agreements are the trap that catches most visitors. Standard car hire contracts from Moab, Grand Junction and Salt Lake City prohibit travel on unpaved roads entirely, and breaching that clause voids your insurance completely — including for damage that has nothing to do with the road surface. If you intend to drive White Rim, Elephant Hill or anything in The Maze, hire from a Moab specialist that rents purpose-equipped 4WDs and permits backcountry use in writing.

  • Two full-size spares for any Maze trip; one, plus a plug kit and compressor, for White Rim.
  • Recovery gear — traction boards, a rated strap, shackles and a shovel.
  • Tyre deflator and compressor. Airing down transforms traction on sand and slickrock.
  • Satellite communicator with two-way messaging and SOS. Not optional anywhere off pavement.
  • Extra fuel in approved cans. Low-range crawling destroys range, and there is no fuel in any district.
  • Paper maps plus downloaded offline mapping. GPS positions work without signal; tiles do not.

Water Strategy and Pack-In Rules

Water is the constraint that shapes every Canyonlands camping trip. There is no potable water at Willow Flat, none at any backcountry site in any district, none on White Rim Road, and none anywhere in The Maze. Squaw Flat has it seasonally and the two visitor centers have it seasonally. That is the complete list.

The planning figure is one gallon — roughly four litres — per person per day for drinking alone, doubled once daytime highs pass 90°F. Cooking, washing and a contingency margin go on top of that. The arithmetic gets heavy quickly, which is why vehicle-based trips carry water in jerry cans and backpackers plan routes around how much they can physically lift.

Water planning by trip type
Trip Party Minimum water Recommended
2 nights, Willow Flat 2 people 16 L 25 L including cooking and washing

3 days, White Rim by 4WD 4 people 48 L 70 L plus a 20 L reserve in the vehicle
4 days, The Maze 2 people 32 L 60 L minimum, plus vehicle coolant reserve

Potholes and seeps do hold water after rain, and springs exist in Salt Creek and a handful of Maze canyons. Do not build a plan around any of them. They are seasonal, unreliable, frequently silted, and — in the case of potholes — fragile ecosystems containing fairy shrimp and tadpoles that die when the water is taken or contaminated. Never dip a bottle, a pan or a swimming body into a pothole. If you draw from a spring as an emergency measure, filter and treat it, and collect downstream of the source.

Warning

Electrolytes, not just water

Drinking large volumes of plain water while sweating heavily in desert heat flushes sodium and can cause hyponatremia, which presents confusingly like heat exhaustion and is more dangerous. On any full day of exertion in Canyonlands, take electrolyte tablets or eat salty food alongside your water. This matters as much as the volume you carry.

Waste, Fires and Leave No Trace

Canyon country recovers from damage on a geological timescale rather than a human one. The rules here are stricter than in most parks, and several of them surprise people who camp regularly elsewhere.

Human waste

A portable toilet system — a wag bag, a groover or an equivalent sealed container — is mandatory for all vehicle-based backcountry camping, including every campsite on White Rim Road and throughout The Maze. Solid human waste must be packed out entirely. Burying it does not work in this environment: the soil is thin, biologically inactive and often nonexistent, and buried waste simply persists.

Backpackers in the Needles zones may in some areas bury waste in a cathole six to eight inches deep, at least two hundred feet from water, campsites and drainages — but pack-out is encouraged everywhere and required in narrow canyons and slickrock zones. Toilet paper is packed out in every case, always, without exception.

Fires

Campfires are permitted only in the metal fire rings at Willow Flat and Squaw Flat, and only with firewood brought in from outside the park. Collecting wood inside Canyonlands is prohibited — dead juniper is habitat and takes centuries to accumulate. Fires are banned entirely in the backcountry in all districts, including in fire pans. Cooking is on a stove. Fire restrictions frequently escalate to a total ban in summer, so confirm current conditions before you travel.

Cryptobiotic soil

The black, lumpy crust between rocks and shrubs is a living community of cyanobacteria, lichen and moss that holds the desert together, fixes nitrogen and prevents erosion. A single footprint destroys decades of growth, and full recovery can take a century or more. Walk on rock, in sandy washes, or on established trails. Pitch tents on slickrock or bare sand. The park's phrase — "don't bust the crust" — is on every sign for a reason.

  • Pack out everything, including food scraps, orange peel and greywater solids.
  • Camp on durable surfaces — slickrock, sand or an established site, never on crust or vegetation.
  • Keep 300 feet from water sources so wildlife can drink; desert springs are lifelines.
  • Wash 200 feet from water, using minimal biodegradable soap and scattering greywater widely.
  • Leave rock art and artefacts untouched. Skin oils destroy pigment; removing objects is a federal offence.
  • Store food securely. Ravens are startlingly capable and will open unattended packs and coolers.

Camping Outside the Park

With thirty-eight sites inside the park, most people camping near Canyonlands are camping outside it. The surrounding public land is extensive and, in places, as good as anything within the boundary.

BLM dispersed camping is free and legal in designated areas on the public land surrounding the park, including along UT-313 on the Island in the Sky approach and along Lockhart Basin Road toward The Needles. There are no facilities of any kind. Many areas around Moab now require a portable toilet system for dispersed camping, and the rule is enforced. Use existing fire rings and pitches rather than creating new ones — dispersed camping degrades landscapes quickly when parties spread out.

BLM developed campgrounds along the Colorado River on UT-128 and near Kane Creek cost around $20 per night, have vault toilets and picnic tables, and are mostly first-come. They are twenty to forty minutes from Moab and considerably easier to secure than anything inside the park.

Dead Horse Point State Park deserves particular mention. It sits on the same mesa as Island in the Sky, a few miles before the park entrance, and its two campgrounds are fully reservable with water and electric hookups. The overlook there is arguably the equal of anything in Canyonlands. For anyone who wants to be on the mesa at dawn without gambling on Willow Flat, it is the strongest option available.

Moab has the full commercial range — private RV parks, glamping outfits and hotels — and is forty minutes from the Island in the Sky trailheads. Monticello is quieter, cheaper and an hour from The Needles. Both make sense as bases; see the driving times table on the hub page for the full comparison.

Desert Camping Gear Checklist

Canyon country punishes gear in specific ways: wind that arrives without warning on exposed mesas, temperature swings of forty degrees between afternoon and dawn, and fine red sand that gets into zips, bearings and lungs.

  • A genuinely freestanding tent with a full fly. Pegs do not hold in sand or slickrock; you will be guying to rocks.
  • Sand stakes or rock anchors plus extra guyline. Wind on the mesa regularly exceeds 30 mph in spring.
  • A three-season sleeping bag even in summer. Willow Flat sits at 6,000 feet and clear desert nights drop hard.
  • An insulated sleeping pad. Slickrock and sand both sink heat fast after dark.
  • Water containers — collapsible jerry cans for vehicles, and more capacity than you think you need.
  • A stove, since backcountry fires are banned everywhere in the park.
  • Shade. A tarp or vehicle awning is close to essential from May onward; natural shade is scarce to nonexistent.
  • Wag bags or a groover for all vehicle-based backcountry camping — mandatory, not advisory.
  • Red-beam headlamp to protect night vision under a Dark Sky Park sky, plus spare batteries.
  • Satellite communicator with two-way messaging and SOS for any backcountry night.
  • A brush and dry bags for sand management. Everything you own will contain red grit by day two.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you reserve campsites in Canyonlands National Park?

Partially. Willow Flat at Island in the Sky is first-come, first-served only — twelve sites, no reservations, ever. Squaw Flat in The Needles takes Recreation.gov reservations for part of its twenty-six sites during spring and autumn, with the rest first-come. All overnight backcountry camping requires an advance Recreation.gov permit with no walk-up option.

How much does a Canyonlands backcountry permit cost in 2026?

A $36 non-refundable reservation fee plus $5 per person per night, booked through Recreation.gov. This applies across every district — Island in the Sky, The Needles and The Maze — and to overnight trips on White Rim Road. It is entirely separate from the $6 day-use permit needed for 4WD, motorcycle or bicycle travel on White Rim Road, Elephant Hill and Lavender Canyon.

Is there water at Canyonlands campgrounds?

Willow Flat has no water at any time of year — carry every drop in. Squaw Flat has seasonal potable water, typically spring through autumn, drained before the first hard freeze. No backcountry site in any district has water, and there is none on White Rim Road or anywhere in The Maze.

Do you need a 4WD to camp in The Maze?

Yes. Access requires a high-clearance four-wheel-drive vehicle with low range, all-terrain tyres and recovery equipment. The Flint Trail switchbacks and everything beyond Hans Flat are technical, unmaintained and impassable when wet. The Park Service recommends two vehicles, two full-size spare tyres and a satellite communicator. Crossover SUVs and rental cars do not qualify.

How far in advance should you book Canyonlands camping?

Backcountry permits open roughly four months ahead and the competitive zones — Chesler Park, Elephant Canyon, the White Rim campsites — go within minutes for spring and autumn weekends. Book Squaw Flat reservable sites the moment the window opens. Willow Flat cannot be booked at all, so arrive before nine in the morning in peak season.

Can you camp for free near Canyonlands National Park?

Yes. Dispersed camping is free on surrounding BLM land, including along UT-313 near the Island in the Sky approach and along Lockhart Basin Road. There are no facilities, many areas around Moab require a portable toilet system, and pack-in pack-out rules are strict. Developed BLM campgrounds along the Colorado River cost around $20 per night.

Are campfires allowed in Canyonlands?

Only in the metal fire rings at Willow Flat and Squaw Flat, using wood brought from outside the park — collecting wood inside Canyonlands is prohibited. Campfires are banned entirely throughout the backcountry in all districts. Summer fire restrictions frequently escalate to a total ban, so check current conditions before travelling.

Can you sleep in your car or van inside Canyonlands?

Only in a paid campground site at Willow Flat or Squaw Flat, or at an assigned backcountry vehicle campsite with a permit. Sleeping in vehicles at overlooks, trailheads, pullouts or visitor center car parks is prohibited throughout the park. Rangers do patrol and will move you on. BLM land outside the boundary is the legal alternative.

What is the best month for camping in Canyonlands?

October, narrowly ahead of April. Autumn brings stable weather, warm rock, comfortable nights and thinner crowds after Labor Day, without the strong afternoon wind that characterises spring. April and May are excellent but windier and busier. Summer nights stay above 65°F with brutal days, and winter brings sub-freezing nights and unreliable dirt-road access.

Are dogs allowed at Canyonlands campgrounds?

Dogs are permitted in the Willow Flat and Squaw Flat campgrounds on a leash no longer than six feet, and on paved roads and in parking areas. They are prohibited on every trail in the park and in all backcountry zones, and may not be left unattended in vehicles or at campsites. Service animals are exempt.
