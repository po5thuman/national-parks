Eye of the Whale Arch: The Willow Springs 4WD Guide

Arches receives 1.8 million visitors a year and almost none of them see this arch. Eye of the Whale sits in Herdina Park at the end of the Willow Springs Road, a deep-sand four-wheel-drive track that turns back most vehicles within the first mile. The reward is genuine solitude inside one of America’s busiest national parks. The cost is that if you get this wrong, you will be digging.

By Daniel Reyes Published Aug 27, 2026 Updated Aug 28, 2026 12 min read

Read This Before You Plan Around It

Willow Springs Road requires a genuine high-clearance four-wheel-drive vehicle. This is not a graded dirt road that a careful driver can manage in a crossover. Sections carry six to twelve inches of loose sand that swallow two-wheel-drive vehicles routinely, and towing recovery from inside the park costs $500–$1,500. Standard rental agreements void insurance the moment you leave pavement. If you do not have the right vehicle, do not attempt this — book a guided 4x4 tour instead.

What Eye of the Whale Actually Is

A modest sandstone arch — roughly 40 feet wide and 12 feet high — set in a fin above the sandy floor of Herdina Park. Seen from the correct angle the opening resolves into an unmistakable eye shape, with the surrounding rock forming the blunt head of a whale breaching the desert. From other angles it is simply a nice arch, which is worth knowing before you drive an hour to see it.

It is not the biggest or the most photogenic span in the park. Landscape Arch is longer, Delicate Arch is more dramatic, Double Arch is more impressive from beneath. What Eye of the Whale has that none of them do is emptiness. On a spring weekday you can spend an hour here and see nobody at all.

Who this trip is for

  • Experienced sand drivers with a suitable vehicle and recovery equipment.
  • Photographers who want a formation nobody has in their feed.
  • Repeat visitors who have already done the main road and want something else.

Who it is not for

  • Anyone in a rental sedan, crossover or all-wheel-drive SUV without low range and real clearance.
  • First-time Arches visitors — see the main hub and do the scenic drive first.
  • Solo travellers without a satellite communicator. There is no phone signal out here.

Note the shape of this trip: it is almost entirely driving. The walk to the arch itself is 0.2 miles on flat sand and takes ten minutes. You are committing two and a half hours to reach a formation you will spend twenty minutes with. That is the correct trade for some people and clearly wrong for others.

Getting There

There are two ways in, and they are not equivalent.

Route A — from US-191 (recommended)

The Willow Springs turn-off leaves US-191 roughly nine miles north of Moab, opposite a gravel pull-out. It is unmarked from the highway — there is no brown park sign — so set your navigation before you lose signal. The road enters BLM land first, then crosses into Arches without a gate or station.

The first two miles are the hardest. This is where the deep sand sits, and where most turned-around vehicles are found. If you clear mile two, the rest of the road is manageable for a capable vehicle.

Route B — from inside the park

Willow Springs Road also connects to the scenic drive near Balanced Rock, at mile 9.2. Coming from this direction you drive the road east to west. It works, but you approach the deep sand section downhill and at the far end of the route — which is precisely where you least want to get stuck, because turning back is no longer an easy option.

We recommend entering from US-191 and, if conditions are good, exiting into the park at Balanced Rock to connect with the scenic drive. That gives you the hard section first, when you still have the option to reverse out.

Never Attempt This Road Wet

Willow Springs Road crosses several washes and sections of bentonite clay. After rain the clay becomes effectively frictionless — four-wheel drive does not help, and vehicles slide off the track. The monsoon season from mid-July through September brings sudden storms that flood the washes within minutes. If rain has fallen in the last 24–48 hours, or if there is any storm visible on the horizon in any direction, do not enter. Rangers close the road after significant rainfall, but the closure is not always signed at the US-191 end.

Driving Deep Sand

Sand driving is a skill, and the instincts most drivers bring to it are wrong. The core principle is momentum and float: you want the vehicle riding on top of the sand rather than digging into it. Almost every mistake below comes from doing the opposite.

Air down your tyres

This is the single most effective thing you can do, and most people skip it. Dropping pressure to 18–20 psi lengthens the tyre footprint and dramatically increases flotation on loose sand. A vehicle that bogs at 35 psi will often walk straight through the same section at 18.

You must carry a means of reinflating. Driving any distance on pavement at 18 psi damages tyres and handles badly. A cheap 12V compressor is the correct piece of equipment and takes about ten minutes to bring four tyres back to highway pressure.

Momentum, not power

Choose a steady speed — typically 10 to 15 mph — and hold it. Do not accelerate hard, do not brake sharply, and do not stop in a sandy section if you can avoid it. Starting from a standstill in deep sand is far harder than maintaining motion through it.

Follow existing tracks

Previous vehicles have compacted the sand in the wheel ruts. Driving in them is easier than breaking new ground, and it also keeps you within the established route — driving off the track inside a national park destroys cryptobiotic soil crust and is a citable offence.

If you start to bog

  • Stop immediately. Continuing to spin the wheels digs you deeper within seconds.
  • Reverse out along your own tracks if you can. This is the most common successful recovery.
  • Drop pressure further — as low as 15 psi — before trying again.
  • Clear sand from in front of all four wheels and create a gentle ramp rather than a wall.
  • Straighten the front wheels. Turned wheels act as ploughs.

What to Carry

There is no phone signal on Willow Springs Road, no water, and on a weekday you may see no other vehicle for hours. Self-sufficiency is not optional here.

Tell Someone Your Plan

Leave your route and expected return time with someone who will act if you do not check in. With no mobile coverage and low traffic, a breakdown here can mean many hours before anyone passes. A satellite communicator with an SOS function is the correct equipment for this road — not a nice-to-have. Do not rely on a phone.

Finding the Arch

Herdina Park is a broad sandy basin ringed by fins. A small pull-out on the north side of the road serves as the parking area — space for perhaps three or four vehicles, usually empty. There is a modest sign, but it is easy to miss if you are concentrating on the driving.

From the pull-out the arch is visible in the fin above you. The walk is roughly 0.2 miles round trip across soft sand with a short rise at the end. Ten minutes at a stroll. There is no maintained trail and none is needed.

Getting the eye

The whale shape only resolves from a specific position — roughly southwest of the arch, looking north-east. From directly beneath, the opening reads as an ordinary span. Walk the base of the fin and watch the shape change until the eye appears. Most people who report being underwhelmed simply never found the angle.

Light

Late afternoon is best. The fin faces broadly west and takes warm light in the last two hours of the day, with the opening picking up reflected glow from the sand below. Morning light is flat here.

One serious caution: if you shoot at sunset, you will drive the road out in the dark. Sand driving at night with headlights only is considerably harder — the texture that tells you where the soft sections are becomes invisible. Either accept that and drive it slowly, or shoot two hours before sunset and leave with light remaining.

Prefer Someone Else Drive?

Several Moab operators run guided 4x4 trips that include Willow Springs Road. You get the terrain without the rental agreement problem, the recovery risk, or the tyre pressure arithmetic.

Other Arches in Herdina Park

The basin holds several more spans that almost nobody visits, and having driven this far you may as well see them.

  • Tower Arch — not in Herdina Park itself but reachable from the Klondike Bluffs area via Salt Valley Road, a 2.6-mile round-trip hike and one of the most satisfying quiet arches in the park.
  • Marching Men — a line of standing rock columns near the Klondike Bluffs junction, visible from the road.
  • Unnamed fins and openings throughout Herdina Park. Wander the basin edges and you will find several small spans that appear on no map.

Leave No Trace Applies Especially Here

Herdina Park sees so few visitors that any impact is disproportionate and lasts. Stay on existing vehicle tracks, walk on sand or slickrock rather than the dark crusted soil between formations, take everything out with you, and do not build cairns. Cryptobiotic crust takes decades to recover from a single footprint.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where is Eye of the Whale Arch?

In Herdina Park, in the western backcountry of Arches National Park, reached via Willow Springs Road. The road leaves US-191 roughly nine miles north of Moab, and the arch sits about 7.8 miles in.

Do you need 4WD for Eye of the Whale Arch?

Yes. Willow Springs Road requires a genuine high-clearance four-wheel-drive vehicle with at least eight inches of clearance. Sections carry six to twelve inches of loose sand. All-wheel-drive crossovers and two-wheel-drive vehicles get stuck here regularly.

Can you hike to Eye of the Whale Arch instead of driving?

Technically yes, but it is a poor idea. You would be walking the full length of Willow Springs Road — around 15 miles round trip through soft sand with no shade and no water. Nobody does this, and in summer it would be genuinely dangerous.

Do you need a permit for Eye of the Whale Arch?

No permit is required. You pay only the standard park entrance fee — $30 per vehicle or an America the Beautiful pass. Unlike Fiery Furnace, there is no booking system for this area.

How long does the trip take?

Two and a half to three and a half hours round trip from Moab, of which almost all is driving. The walk from the parking pull-out to the arch is only 0.2 miles round trip.

Is Willow Springs Road open in winter?

Usually, but conditions govern. Snow and freeze-thaw cycles turn the clay sections to mud, and the road closes after significant precipitation. Winter also brings very short daylight, which matters on a route you should not drive in the dark. Spring and autumn are far better.

What happens if I get stuck?

You self-recover, or you wait. There is no mobile signal, commercial towing into the park costs $500–$1,500, and rental insurance will not cover it because you left pavement. Carry recovery boards, a shovel, a compressor and a satellite communicator — or book a guided tour instead.
