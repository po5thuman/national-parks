Las Vegas to Zion & Bryce Canyon: The Complete Driving Itinerary

Harry Reid International (LAS) is the closest practical airport to Zion National Park — 2 hours 40 minutes and 165 miles of straightforward interstate. This is the full route from the rental counter to Bryce Canyon’s rim, including the tunnel restriction that catches RV renters and the timing that decides whether you sit in the Springdale shuttle queue.

By Daniel Reyes Published Aug 15, 2026 Updated Aug 28, 2026 16 min read

The Route in One Line

LAS → I-15 north → Springdale (Zion), 165 mi / 2h 40m → SR-9 east through the Mount Carmel tunnel → US-89 north → SR-12 → Bryce Canyon, 86 mi / 1h 50m. Total driving from Las Vegas to Bryce Canyon via Zion is 251 miles. Minimum viable trip: four days. Comfortable: five to six.

Which Airport Is Closest to Zion National Park?

Harry Reid International in Las Vegas is the answer for almost every visitor, but it is worth understanding why — because two closer airports exist and both are usually the wrong choice.

Airports serving Zion National Park — distance, drive time and practical assessment
Airport Code To Springdale Drive Time Assessment
St. George Regional SGU 42 mi 50 min Closest, but few routes and high fares
Cedar City Regional CDC 60 mi 1h 10m Very limited service, SLC connections only
Harry Reid International LAS 165 mi 2h 40m Best overall — fares, rentals, frequency
Salt Lake City International SLC 310 mi 4h 30m Only if combining with northern Utah parks

SGU is genuinely closer and if a convenient fare appears, take it. In practice it serves a handful of regional routes at premium prices, and rental inventory is thin enough that one-way drop-offs are often unavailable. LAS wins on fare competition, 24-hour rental desks and the sheer number of direct flights.

Note on the airport’s name

Las Vegas’s airport was renamed from McCarran International to Harry Reid International in 2021. The IATA code remains LAS. Older guidebooks, rental confirmations and signage still use McCarran — they refer to the same place.

The Springdale-to-Mount-Carmel leg looks trivial at 24 miles and takes 45 minutes. That is not traffic — it is the switchbacks and the tunnel. Plan accordingly.

Mount Carmel Tunnel Size Restriction

The Zion–Mount Carmel Tunnel on SR-9 was built in 1930 and cannot accommodate two-way traffic for large vehicles. Any vehicle over 7′10″ wide or 11′4″ tall requires a tunnel escort permit (currently $15, valid two trips) and can only pass during escort hours. This catches RV and camper-van renters constantly. Vehicles over 13′1″ tall or 40′ long are prohibited entirely and must detour via I-15 and US-89 — adding roughly two hours.

Day 1 — Las Vegas to Springdale

Collect the car and get out of the metro area before committing to a supermarket stop. The I-15 corridor through North Las Vegas is congested until roughly Exit 64; the Smith’s in Mesquite at mile 80 is a far calmer place to stock up, and it is the last full-size grocery before Springdale’s small and expensive market.

The Virgin River Gorge

Between mile 100 and 120 the interstate drops into the Virgin River Gorge in the far northwest corner of Arizona — a genuinely spectacular fifteen-minute stretch that most drivers do not expect. There are two pull-outs. Take one.

Arrive Springdale before 4 p.m. if you can

Springdale is a single street. Check in, then walk to the pedestrian entrance rather than driving into the park — Zion’s in-park parking fills before 9 a.m. in season and the town shuttle runs the length of the street to the gate.

Day 2 — Zion National Park

The Zion Canyon Scenic Drive is closed to private vehicles from March through late November. Everything up canyon — The Grotto, Weeping Rock, Temple of Sinawava — is shuttle-only. The first shuttle leaves around 6 a.m. in peak season and queues build fast after 8 a.m.

If you have an Angels Landing permit

Take the earliest shuttle you can. The chain section faces east and takes direct sun from mid-morning; the descent is also considerably less pleasant with two-way traffic on the chains after 10 a.m.

If you do not

Scout’s Lookout — the point where the Angels Landing chains begin — requires no permit and delivers perhaps 80% of the view. Observation Point via the East Mesa trailhead is the other strong option: higher than Angels Landing, no permit, and a fraction of the foot traffic.

Day 3 — Zion to Bryce Canyon

This is the leg people underestimate. It is 86 miles, and the driving alone is under two hours — but the Zion–Mount Carmel Highway is one of the best roads in the American Southwest and rushing it is a waste of the day. Budget four to five hours door to door.

The switchbacks and the tunnel

Leaving Springdale, SR-9 climbs a series of tight switchbacks to the tunnel entrance. Once inside, the 1.1-mile tunnel has a handful of gallery windows cut into the cliff face — you cannot stop, but they are worth watching for. Emerging on the east side puts you in an entirely different landscape: pale slickrock domes and Checkerboard Mesa rather than the red canyon walls behind you.

Canyon Overlook Trail

Immediately past the eastern tunnel portal is the Canyon Overlook trailhead — one mile round trip, 160 feet of gain, and a view straight down the length of Zion Canyon that rivals far harder hikes. Parking is limited to about a dozen spaces and turns over quickly.

Red Canyon on SR-12

After Mount Carmel Junction, US-89 north is unremarkable until you turn east onto SR-12 and pass through the twin arches cut into Red Canyon’s vermillion rock. It is a five-minute stretch and the most photographed non-park road segment in the state.

Day 4 — Bryce Canyon National Park

Bryce is small and concentrated. The main amphitheatre viewpoints — Sunrise, Sunset, Inspiration and Bryce Point — sit within three miles of each other, and the essential hike connects two of them.

The Queens Garden and Navajo Loop combination

Three miles, 600 feet of descent and re-ascent, and the single best short hike in southern Utah. Go down Queens Garden and up Navajo — the reverse means climbing the steeper, sun-exposed side. Allow two to three hours including stops.

Altitude is the thing nobody warns you about

Bryce’s rim sits at 8,000 to 9,100 feet. If you flew into Las Vegas at 2,000 feet two days earlier, you have gained 7,000 feet in 72 hours. Headaches and breathlessness on the Navajo climb are common and usually just altitude. Drink more water than feels necessary and take the ascent slowly.

Stay for the night sky

Bryce is an International Dark Sky Park with some of the lowest light pollution in the contiguous United States. On a moonless night the Milky Way is visible to the naked eye from the Sunset Point parking area with no equipment at all. This is a genuine reason to book a second night rather than driving out.

Day 5 — Return, or Extend

The direct return to Las Vegas is 260 miles and about 4 hours 10 minutes via SR-12, US-89 and I-15 south. If your flight is after 6 p.m. you can leave Bryce at 9 a.m. and still have margin.

Worthwhile extensions

  • Kolob Canyons — Zion’s northwest section, its own I-15 exit (Exit 40), rarely crowded, and directly on the return route. Adds 90 minutes.
  • Cedar Breaks National Monument — a smaller Bryce-like amphitheatre at 10,000 feet. Closed by snow October to May. Adds two hours.
  • Valley of Fire State Park — 50 minutes northeast of Las Vegas, spectacular, and a strong final stop if you have a late flight. Adds three hours.

Practical Notes

Rental car considerations

A standard passenger car handles this entire route. You do not need 4WD or high clearance for Zion or Bryce. If you rent an RV or camper van, check the dimensions against the tunnel restriction above before you book — this is the most common trip-planning failure on this route.

Fuel

Fill up in Hurricane or St. George before Springdale, and again at Mount Carmel Junction before the US-89 stretch. Springdale fuel carries a significant premium and the stretch between Mount Carmel and Bryce has limited options.

Time zones

Las Vegas is Pacific Time; Utah is Mountain Time. You lose an hour crossing the state line and gain it back on return. Every year visitors miss return flights by forgetting this on the way out.

Park passes

Zion and Bryce each charge $35 per vehicle for seven days. The America the Beautiful annual pass is $80 and pays for itself on this trip alone. Buy it at the first entrance station.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the closest airport to Zion National Park?

St. George Regional (SGU) is closest at 42 miles, but service is limited. Harry Reid International in Las Vegas (LAS) is 165 miles and about 2 hours 40 minutes, and is the practical choice for nearly every visitor on fares, rental availability and flight frequency.

How long is the drive from Las Vegas to Zion National Park?

165 miles and roughly 2 hours 40 minutes via I-15 north and SR-9 east, arriving in Springdale at the park’s south entrance. Allow extra time for the Virgin River Gorge pull-outs and a grocery stop in Mesquite.

How far is Zion to Bryce Canyon?

86 miles, about 1 hour 50 minutes of pure driving via SR-9 east through the Mount Carmel Tunnel, US-89 north and SR-12 east. Budget four to five hours in practice — the Zion–Mount Carmel Highway, Canyon Overlook Trail and Red Canyon all deserve stops.

How far is Las Vegas to Bryce Canyon?

251 miles via Zion, or 260 miles and about 4 hours 10 minutes on the direct return route via SR-12, US-89 and I-15 south. Driving Las Vegas to Bryce Canyon as a day trip is possible but not advisable — it is eight hours of driving for a few hours on the rim.

Can you drive an RV from Zion to Bryce Canyon?

Only with attention to the tunnel. Vehicles over 7′10″ wide or 11′4″ tall need a $15 escort permit and can pass only during escort hours. Vehicles over 13′1″ tall or 40′ long are banned outright and must detour via I-15 and US-89, adding roughly two hours.

How many days do you need for Las Vegas, Zion and Bryce Canyon?

Four days is the workable minimum: one to drive and settle, one in Zion, one to transit and see Bryce’s viewpoints, one to return. Five or six is far better, allowing two full days in Zion and a night under Bryce’s dark skies.

Do you need a permit for this road trip?

Not for the drive itself. Angels Landing in Zion requires a lottery permit, and the Mount Carmel Tunnel requires an escort permit for oversized vehicles. Bryce Canyon needs no permits for day hiking.
